# §3 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: When a disease has shivering aversion to cold followed again by fever, what is it? Answer: When the yin pulse is insufficient, yang goes and follows it; when the yang pulse is insufficient, yin goes and assails it. Question: What is meant by yang insufficiency? Answer: Suppose the cun kou pulse is faint—this is called yang insufficiency; yin qi rises and enters into the yang, and there is shivering aversion to cold. Question: What is meant by yin insufficiency? Answer: When the chi pulse is weak, it is called yin insufficiency; yang qi sinks down and enters into the yin, and there is fever. When the yang pulse is floating (one version writes "faint") and the yin pulse weak, the blood is vacuous; when the blood is vacuous, the sinews are tense. When the pulse is sunken, the nutritive qi is faint. When the pulse is floating and sweat comes out like rolling pearls, the defensive qi is debilitated. When one whose nutritive qi is faint is given a burning needle, the blood stagnates and does not move, and there are further fever and agitation and vexation.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

One yin and one yang is called the Way; leaning toward yin or toward yang is called disease. When yin is one-sidedly insufficient, yang can follow it; when yang is one-sidedly insufficient, yin can assail it. When yang is insufficient, yin qi rises into the yang causing aversion to cold, because when yin prevails there is cold. When yin is insufficient, yang qi sinks into the yin causing fever, because when yang prevails there is heat. When the yang pulse is floating and the yin pulse weak, the blood is vacuous, and blood vacuity makes the sinews tense. Yang is qi; yin is blood. A floating yang pulse means the defensive qi is strong; a weak yin pulse means the nutritive blood is weak. The *Nanjing* says: "Qi governs warming them; blood governs moistening them." When blood is vacuous it cannot moisten the sinews and network vessels, so the sinews are tense. [The commentary's "皿虚" is read as "血虚" (blood vacuity).]

The *Neijing* says: "The vessels are the storehouse of blood." When the pulse is replete the blood is replete; when the pulse is vacuous the blood is vacuous; this is the norm. A sunken pulse shows that the nutritive blood is faint within.

The *Zhenjing* says: "The defensive qi is what warms the parting flesh, fills the skin and hair, fattens the interstices, and governs opening and closing." A floating pulse with sweat like rolling pearls means the interstices are not dense and opening and closing are not governed; the defensive qi is debilitated outside. Floating governs examining the defensive, sunken governs examining the nutritive; distinguishing the debility and faintness of nutritive and defensive by floating and sunken is naturally reasonable. But debility is more severe than faintness. "Faint" is said of the nutritive and "debilitated" of the defensive because sweat like rolling pearls is the outward desertion of yang qi, so the disease of the defensive is more severe than that of the nutritive.

The defensive is yang; the nutritive is yin. A burning needle boosts yang and damages yin. "Faint nutritive qi" means yin vacuity. The *Neijing* says: "Yin vacuity produces internal heat." When there is internal heat and a burning needle is further applied to supplement yang, not only do the two heats combine so that the nutritive blood does not move, but there will certainly be further external fever and internal agitation and vexation.

## Notes

The clause explains the mechanism of alternating aversion to cold and fever by insufficiency of the cun kou (yang) and chi (yin) pulses, and goes on to the pulse signs of vacuity of the nutritive (blood) and defensive (qi), and the contraindication of the burning needle. It shows the idea that when one of yin and yang is insufficient, the other intrudes there.
