# §10 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

When yin and yang contend, it is called stirred. When yang stirs there is sweating; when yin stirs there is fever. When the body is cold with aversion to cold, the triple burner is damaged. If a rapid pulse is seen at the guan, without head or tail above and below, the size of a bean, throbbing and shaking, it is called stirred.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

Stirred is yin and yang contending. When yin and yang contend and are vacuous, there is stirring. Yang stirring is yang vacuity, so there is sweating; yin stirring is yin vacuity, so there is fever. If there is neither sweating nor fever but, on the contrary, the body is cold with aversion to cold, the triple burner is damaged. The triple burner is the separate envoy of the source qi and governs moving qi in the yang; once the triple burner is damaged, yang qi does not flow and becomes faint, so the body becomes cold with aversion to cold. The *Jingui Yaolüe* says: "When yang qi does not flow, the body is cold." The classic says: "When yang is faint, there is aversion to cold."

The *Maijing* says: "Yang goes out and yin comes in, with the guan as the boundary." The guan is the middle of yin and yang. If a rapid pulse is seen at the guan, without head or tail above and below, the size of a bean, throbbing and shaking, the qi of yin and yang is contending; therefore it is called stirred.

## Notes

The clause defines the "stirred pulse" (a pulse name: a short pulse jumping like a bean at the guan position). It arises from yin and yang contending, and is said to be accompanied by sweating when it appears at the cun (yang) and fever when at the chi (yin).
