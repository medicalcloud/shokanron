# §32 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

When the cun kou pulse is tight at both yin and yang, by rule clear pathogen has struck the upper jiao and turbid pathogen has struck the lower jiao. Clear pathogen striking above is called clean; turbid pathogen striking below is called muddy. When yin is struck by pathogen, there will certainly be internal shuddering; the exterior qi is slightly deficient and the interior qi does not guard, so the pathogen strikes the yin. When yang is struck by pathogen, there will certainly be fever, headache, stiff nape, neck spasm, lumbar pain, and aching shins, because yang has been struck by the qi of mist and dew; hence it is said: clear pathogen strikes above, turbid pathogen strikes below. Yin qi produces shuddering, counterflow cold of the feet and knees, and reckless discharge of stool and urine. The exterior qi is slightly deficient and the interior qi slightly urgent; the sanjiao becomes muddled together and inside and outside do not communicate. When the upper jiao is depressed (*fu*, pronounced *fo*; likewise below) and the visceral qi fumes, the mouth ulcerates and the gums are eroded. When the middle jiao is not governed, stomach qi surges upward, spleen qi does not turn, the stomach becomes turbid, the ying-nutrient and wei-defense do not flow, and blood congeals and does not move. If the wei-defensive qi flows through first, the urine is red-yellow; it contends with the heat, and acting as an envoy through the heat, roams the meridians and collaterals and goes in and out of the zang-fu organs; where the heat qi passes, there are welling-abscesses with pus. If the yin qi flows through first, the yang qi is reversed and feeble, yin has nothing to employ, guest qi enters within and is expelled by sneezing, the voice is choked (*wa*, pronounced yi-gu) and the throat obstructed; cold and cold limbs chase each other, are hemmed in by heat, and congealed blood descends of itself, looking like pig's liver. When yin and yang are both reversed, the spleen qi is isolated and weak, the five fluids pour downward, the lower jiao does not close (one version writes 闔), there are bowel movements with rectal heaviness, making defecation frequent and difficult, and the navel pounds with gripping pain; life will hardly be preserved.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

Floating is yang; deep is yin. When the yang pulse is tight, the qi of mist and dew has struck the upper jiao; when the yin pulse is tight, pathogenic cold has struck the lower jiao. The upper jiao is the Taiyang; the lower jiao is the Shaoyin. Fever, headache, stiff nape, neck spasm, lumbar pain, and aching shins mean the qi of mist and dew has struck the Taiyang meridian. Turbid pathogen striking below, with yin qi producing shuddering, counterflow cold of the feet and shins, and reckless discharge of stool and urine, means pathogenic cold has struck the Shaoyin. Because the exterior qi is slightly deficient, the pathogen enters and lodges there; and because the interior qi does not guard, the pathogen takes advantage of the interior weakness and finally strikes the yin. When yin is deficient and meets the pathogen, there is internal fear and shuddering, leading to slight urgency of qi. The *Neijing* says: "Yang disease goes up to the extreme and then descends; yin disease goes down to the extreme and then ascends." Here, when the pathogen of the upper jiao is severe, it descends to invade the middle jiao; when the pathogen of the lower jiao is severe, it ascends to invade the middle jiao; thereby the sanjiao becomes muddled and disordered. The sanjiao governs and holds all qi; once it is muddled and disordered, the qi of inside and outside cannot communicate. The *danzhong* is the sea of yang qi; since the qi cannot communicate inside and outside, it is depressed in the upper jiao and becomes heat, fuming with the zang organs, so the mouth ulcerates and the gums are eroded. The *Neijing* says: "When the diaphragm is hot and [the bowels] do not move, it rises to become erosion of the mouth." The middle jiao is where the pathogens of the upper and lower jiao are muddled, so it cannot be calmly governed. The middle jiao is within the stomach; when the middle jiao loses its governance, the stomach qi surges upward. The spleen is *kun*; *kun* assists the stomach qi to grind water and grain. When the spleen qi does not turn, the water and grain in the stomach are not ground and digested, so the stomach is turbid. The *Jingui Yaolüe* says: "When grain qi is not dispersed, the stomach suffers from turbidity." The ying-nutrient is the essential qi of water and grain; the wei-defense is the fierce qi of water and grain. When qi cannot spread and disperse, the ying-nutrient and wei-defense do not flow and blood congeals and does not move. The wei-defensive qi is yang qi; the ying-nutrient blood is yin qi. Yang governs heat; yin governs cold. "The wei-defensive qi flowing through first" means the yang qi flows through first and the heat qi is able to move. The *Neijing* says: "The bladder stores the fluids; when qi transformation occurs, they can come out." From the urine being red-yellow, one knows the wei-defensive qi has flowed through first. The heat qi contends with the stomach qi and moves, going in and out of the zang-fu organs and roaming the meridians and collaterals. When heat lodges in the meridians and collaterals, blood congeals and flesh putrefies into welling-abscesses with pus; this shows that the heat qi has been able to move. If the yin qi flows through first, it is otherwise. Yang is outside as the envoy of yin; because the yang qi is reversed and feeble, yin has nothing to employ, and so the yin qi flows through first. The *Neijing* says: "Yang qi guards the outside and makes it secure." When the yang qi is reversed and feeble, it cannot guard the outside, and cold qi accordingly lodges there. The nose is where the lung is observed, and the lung governs the voice; cold qi entering within lodges in the lung meridian, so there is sneezing to expel it, the voice is choked and the throat obstructed. Cold is the outer pathogen; cold limbs is the inner pathogen. The pathogens of outside and inside combine and chase each other. When it becomes heat, blood congeals and does not move; now, being hemmed in by heat, the blood is made to congeal and descend of itself, like pig's liver. When the yang qi of the upper jiao is reversed and the yin qi of the lower jiao is reversed, and the two qi are both reversed and do not connect smoothly, the spleen qi alone is weak and cannot move and transform qi and blood to nourish the five zang organs, so the five zang organs are all deficient and the five fluids pour downward. The *Zhenjing* says: "When the five zang organs are not harmonized, the fluids overflow and flow down into the yin." *He* means closing. *Qing* is *qing* (privy). The qi of the lower jiao deserts and does not close, so there are frequent bowel movements with rectal heaviness. The navel is the source of the generative qi; when the navel pounds with gripping pain, the generative qi is about to be cut off; therefore it is said that life will hardly be preserved.

## Notes

One of the longest and most difficult clauses of the "Distinguishing the Pulse" chapter. It begins with the qi of mist and dew (clear pathogen) striking the upper jiao and cold-pathogenic damp (turbid pathogen) striking the lower jiao, and describes the pathological changes arising in each of the upper, middle, and lower jiao from the disorder of triple-jiao qi, as well as the outcomes when yang qi flows through first (abscesses) and when yin qi flows through first (bloody discharge, etc.). There are many textual and punctuation problems, and interpretations vary. "斉" is understood to mean "臍" (navel).
