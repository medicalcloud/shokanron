# §1 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: The pulse has yin and yang—what does this mean? Answer: In general, large, floating, rapid, stirred, and slippery pulses are called yang; deep, hesitant, weak, wiry, and feeble pulses are called yin. In general, when a yang pulse is seen in yin disease, one lives; when a yin pulse is seen in yang disease, one dies.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

The *Neijing* says: "Subtlety lies in the pulse; it must be examined. Examining it has its guiding principle—it begins from yin and yang. Beginning it has its constant—it arises from the five phases." The pulse's yin and yang are discussed first here because the pulse begins from yin and yang. There are five yang pulses and five yin pulses because the pulse arises from the five phases. The way of yang is always in surplus: the five—large, floating, rapid, stirred, and slippery—are in surplus compared with the normal pulse, so they are called yang. The way of yin is always lacking: the five—deep, hesitant, weak, wiry, and feeble—fall short of the normal pulse, so they are called yin. In cold damage disease, when the pathogen is in the exterior, yang pulses are seen; when the pathogen is in the interior, yin pulses are seen. A yang pulse seen in yin disease governs life because the pathogenic qi is moving from the interior to the exterior and is about to resolve through sweating—as in "Jueyin wind attack with a slightly floating pulse is about to recover; when not floating, it has not yet recovered." A yin pulse seen in yang disease governs death because the pathogenic qi has entered from the exterior into the interior, the healthy qi is deficient and the pathogen prevails—as in "delirium with a deep thready pulse is fatal." The *Jingui Yaolüe* says: "Of all diseases, those outside can be treated; those that have entered the interior are fatal at once." This is what is meant.

## Notes

The first passage of the chapter "Distinguishing the Pulse," regarded as the general statement dividing pulses into yin and yang.

- Large, floating, rapid, stirred, and slippery are yang pulses (surplus); deep, hesitant, weak, wiry, and feeble are yin pulses (insufficiency).
- It gives the prognostic principle that a yang pulse appearing in yin disease is a favorable sign of the disease moving outward, while a yin pulse appearing in yang disease is an unfavorable sign of the disease sinking inward.
- The two chapters "Distinguishing the Pulse" and "Balancing the Pulse" deal with pulse study; it has long been held that they are later additions by Wang Shuhe or others.
