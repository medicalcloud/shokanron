# §25 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

In one without dual contraction of cold, without further transmission through the channels, and without the addition of a different qi: on the seventh day taiyang disease declines and the headache slightly improves; on the eighth day yangming disease declines and the body heat slightly abates; on the ninth day shaoyang disease declines and the deafness gives way to slight hearing; on the tenth day taiyin disease declines, the abdomen reduces to normal, and one thinks of food and drink; on the eleventh day shaoyin disease declines, thirst stops, the dryness of the tongue ends, and one sneezes; on the twelfth day jueyin disease declines, the scrotum relaxes, the lower abdomen eases slightly, the great qi all departs, and the patient's spirit is clear. If after the thirteenth day or more there is no improvement and the cun and chi pulses are sunken, it is very dangerous. If a different qi is further contracted and it transforms into another disease, treat according to the later spoiled-disease patterns. If the pulse is exuberant at both yin and yang and cold is contracted again, it transforms into warm malaria. When the yang pulse is floating and slippery and the yin pulse soft and weak, and wind is further encountered, it transforms into wind warmth. When the yang pulse is surging and rapid and the yin pulse replete and large, and warm heat is further encountered, it transforms into warm toxin; warm toxin is the heaviest disease. When the yang pulse is soft and weak and the yin pulse wiry and tight, and warm qi is further encountered, it transforms into warm pestilence (one version writes "malaria"). Thus for warm disease arising from damage by cold in winter, the changes of the pulse, the signs, and the formulas and treatments are as explained.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

In six days [the disease] passes through all three yin and three yang; [from the seventh to the twelfth day] the qi all harmonize, the qi of the great evil all departs, and the patient's spirit is clear.

*Jian* means healing. When the transmission through the channels is complete in twelve days, it should heal. If after the thirteenth day or more it has not healed and the chi and cun pulses are sunken, the upright qi is declining within and the evil qi alone prevails; hence it is called very dangerous. [The commentary's "巳上" is read as "已上."]

A different qi means that while the earlier disease has not yet recovered, another, different qi is contracted. The two evils combine and transform into another disease. A pulse exuberant at both yin and yang is the pulse of cold damage. The *Nanjing* says: "The pulse of cold damage is exuberant at both yin and yang and tight and rough." The classic says: "A exuberant pulse with a cold body is obtained from cold damage." This is while the heat of the earlier disease has not yet recovered, cold is contracted again; cold and heat contend and transform into warm malaria.

This is while the earlier heat has not yet abated, wind is contracted again. The *Nanjing* says: "The pulse of wind strike is floating and slippery at yang and soft and weak at yin." Wind comes and assails the heat, so it transforms into wind warmth.

This is while the earlier heat has not yet recovered, warm heat is contracted again. Yang governs the exterior and yin the interior; surging, rapid, replete, and large are all heat. The two heats combine and transform into warm toxin. Because both exterior and interior are hot, it is the heaviest disease.

This is while the earlier heat has not yet recovered, warm qi is contracted again. Warmth and heat combine and transform into warm pestilence.

## Notes

The clause describes the course in which cold damage without dual contraction declines in order from taiyang to jueyin from the seventh to the twelfth day and recovers (based on the *Suwen* "Treatise on Heat"), and how, if it does not recover and another qi is contracted, it transforms into warm malaria, wind warmth, warm toxin, warm pestilence, and so on. It is regarded as one source of the disease names of later warm-disease studies.
