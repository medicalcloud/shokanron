# §1 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Regarding the methods of treating cold damage, having surveyed the formula books of the various schools, the one who has grasped the most of Zhongjing is only Sun Simiao. Yet [even he] says: "I see the court physicians treating cold damage only by administering cold things such as daqing and zhimu, which is utterly contrary to Zhongjing's original intent." And he also says: "Seeking the general sense of the formulas, they are no more than three kinds: first Guizhi, second Mahuang, third Qinglong; all treatment of cold damage does not go beyond these." Alas! This is not yet knowing the depth of the method. Zhongjing's intention, for diseases arising in yang, is to use things like guizhi, shengjiang, and dazao; for those arising in yin, things like ganjiang, gancao, and fuzi. It does not mean using warm and hot medicines throughout; rather, it adopts the *Suwen* teaching that acrid and sweet effuse and dissipate. Moreover, wind and cold cannot be effused and dissipated except by acrid and sweet. Further, for wind strike with spontaneous sweating, Guizhi is used; for cold damage without sweating, Mahuang is used; for wind strike showing a cold pulse or cold damage showing a wind pulse, Qinglong is used. One who wishes to treat cold damage without knowing this has not yet found the gate. Thus these three formulas are suitable for use in spring and winter; in summer and autumn, diseases are mostly summerheat stroke, and Baihu should be used. Therefore the *Great Treatise on Yin and Yang* says: "An exuberant pulse with a cold body is contracted from cold damage; a vacuous pulse with a hot body is contracted from summerheat damage." It also says: "In the fifth and sixth months the yang qi is already exuberant; broken by cold, the heat of the disease is heavy." The *Separate Treatise* says: "Taiyang heat strike is summerheat stroke; the person sweats with aversion to cold, has a hot body, and is thirsty; Baihu governs it." If Guizhi, Mahuang, and the like are mistakenly taken, none has failed to turn yellow, break out in macules, lose blood, and yet survive. This is something the ancients had not reached, and so it is appended at the end of the scroll.

## Notes

The "Afterword" appended at the end of the Song-edition *Shanghan lun*. Its author is not named. There is no Cheng Wuji commentary.

- It criticizes Sun Simiao's summary, in the *Qianjin fang*, of the treatment of cold damage as the three formulas Guizhi, Mahuang, and Qinglong, as not grasping the depth of Zhongjing's method.
- It sets out the threefold distinction of wind strike (Guizhi tang), cold damage (Mahuang tang), and wind strike with a cold pulse / cold damage with a wind pulse (Da Qinglong tang), which can be seen as a forerunner of the later "three-pillar" theory.
- It argues that by season, Guizhi, Mahuang, and Qinglong are to be used in spring and winter, and Baihu tang for summerheat stroke in summer and autumn, and that mistakenly using Guizhi or Mahuang for summerheat stroke does grave harm.
- The quotation from the *Great Treatise on Yin and Yang* corresponds to text in the "Examples of Cold Damage" chapter, and the quotation from the *Separate Treatise* to text in the chapter on tetany, dampness, and summerheat stroke.
