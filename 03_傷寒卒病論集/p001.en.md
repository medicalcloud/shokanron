# §1 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

The treatise says: Every time I read of how Yueren (Bian Que) entered Guo and examined [the crown prince], and how he inspected the complexion of the Marquis of Qi, I cannot help sighing with deep feeling at his outstanding talent. What is strange is that the scholars of today do not pay the least attention to medicine and drugs or study the methods of the art thoroughly—above, to treat the illnesses of their lords and parents; below, to relieve the distress of the poor and lowly; in the middle, to preserve their own bodies whole for long, nourishing their lives—but merely compete in pursuing glory and power, stand on tiptoe looking up to the powerful and rich, bustle and hustle, devoting themselves only to fame and profit, honoring and adorning the branches while neglecting and discarding the root, making the outside splendid while the inside withers. If the skin does not exist, where will the hair attach? Suddenly encountering the qi of evil wind and contracting an extraordinary illness, when suffering comes and disaster arrives, only then do they tremble in fear, lower their ambitions and bend their principles, look up reverently to shamans and invocators, declare their extremity and resign themselves to Heaven, and fold their hands to accept defeat. Carrying a lifespan of a hundred years and holding the most precious vessel, they entrust it to ordinary physicians and let them do as they please. Alas, alas! Their bodies have already perished, their spirit is extinguished, they are transformed into other things, hidden deep in the Yellow Springs, and [those left behind] merely weep in vain. How painful! The whole world is in a stupor, none can awaken; they do not cherish their lives but treat life lightly like this—what then is that glory and power worth? Advancing, they cannot love others or know others; retreating, they cannot love themselves or know themselves; meeting disaster and encountering calamity, they find themselves in a perilous place, dull and benighted, wriggling like wandering souls. How lamentable! The scholars who rush after the world race and compete in empty splendor, do not secure the root, forget their bodies in pursuit of things, are as endangered as one on thin ice or at a deep valley—and come to this! My clan was originally large, formerly over two hundred people. Since the beginning of the Jian'an era, not yet ten years, two out of three have died, and cold damage accounted for seven out of ten of them. Moved by the past ruin and loss, and grieved that untimely and premature deaths could not be saved, I diligently sought the ancient teachings, widely gathered the many formulas, selected and used the *Suwen*, the *Nine Scrolls*, the *Eighty-One Difficulties*, the *Great Treatise on Yin and Yang*, and the *Tailu yaolu*, together with pulse assessment and pattern differentiation, and made the *Shanghan zabing lun* in sixteen scrolls in all. Though it cannot cure all diseases, perhaps it will enable one to see a disease and know its source. If one can search out what I have collected, one will grasp more than half. Heaven spreads the five phases to move the myriad kinds; humans receive the five constants and so have the five viscera; through the channels and network vessels and the bowel points, yin and yang meet and communicate; they are mysterious and subtle, and their transformations are hard to fathom. Unless one's talent is high and insight subtle, how could one explore their principles? In high antiquity there were Shennong, Huangdi, Qibo, Bogao, Leigong, Shaoyu, Shaoshi, and Zhongwen; in the middle ages there were Changsang and Bian Que; in the Han there were Gongcheng Yangqing and Canggong; after them I have heard of none. Looking at today's physicians, they do not think to seek the meaning of the classics to extend what they know; each inherits the family technique and from beginning to end follows the old ways. In examining illness and asking about disease, they strive only for glib answers; facing [the patient] for a moment, they promptly prescribe decoctions. They press the cun but do not reach the chi; they grasp the hand but do not reach the foot; renying and fuyang—the three positions—are not compared; counting the pulse beats against the breaths, they do not reach fifty; without knowing the short term [of death] they decide the diagnosis; of the nine indicators there is not even a semblance; the bright hall and the palace gate (regions of the face) are not observed at all. This is what is called peering through a tube. To discern death and distinguish life is truly difficult! Confucius said: "Those born knowing it are the highest; those who learn it are next; much hearing and broad knowledge are the next level of knowing." I have long esteemed the medical arts; I ask to devote myself to these words.

## Notes

The text regarded as Zhang Zhongjing's own preface. There is no Cheng Wuji commentary.

- The first part laments the tendency of contemporary scholars to pursue fame and profit while neglecting medicine, entrusting their lives to shamans and mediocre physicians once they fall ill.
- The middle part gives his motive for writing: in less than ten years from the Jian'an era (from 196), two-thirds of his clan of over two hundred died, seven-tenths of them from cold damage.
- He names the works consulted—the *Suwen*, the *Nine Scrolls* (said to correspond to the *Lingshu*), the *Eighty-One Difficulties* (*Nanjing*), the *Great Treatise on Yin and Yang*, and the *Tailu yaolu*—and states that he wrote the *Shanghan zabing lun* in sixteen scrolls.
- The latter part criticizes the carelessness of contemporary physicians who do not perform pulse diagnosis thoroughly.
- Some hold that the latter part of the preface (from "Heaven spreads the five phases" onward) is a later addition.
- "嬰非常之疾，患及禍至" is also punctuated by some as "嬰非常之疾患，及禍至" ("contracting an extraordinary illness, and when disaster arrives").
