# §1 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: The pulse has three positions; yin and yang assail each other; the nutritive and defensive, blood and qi, are in the human body; with breathing they go in and out, rising and falling within; by the breath they roam and spread; the fluids flow and circulate; they move according to the season, imitating its forms—wiry in spring, floating in autumn, sunken in winter, surging in summer. Examining the complexion and observing the pulse, sizes differ; within a single period there are changes without constancy; the chi and cun are uneven, sometimes short, sometimes long; above and below are at odds, sometimes present, sometimes absent; disease suddenly changes, advancing and retreating, low and high; the heart is confused and the mind bewildered, and one is apt to lose the guiding principles. Please explain in detail so that it may be made clear. The master said: What you ask is the root of the Way. The pulse has three positions: chi, cun, and guan. The nutritive and defensive flow without losing the balance of the scale. Kidney sunken, heart surging, lung floating, liver wiry—these are the constants, without the slightest deviation. Going out and in, rising and falling, circulating with the water clock; in the time the water falls a hundred marks, they make one complete cycle and return to the cun kou, where vacuity and repletion are seen. In changes they assail each other, yin and yang invade each other: wind makes it floating and vacuous; cold makes it firm and hard; sunken and submerged means collected water; propping rheum makes it urgent and wiry; stirred means pain; rapid means heat and vexation. If there is a lack of correspondence, know what the change stems from; when the three positions differ, diseases each have different origins. Excess is suspicious; insufficiency likewise. Evil does not appear in vain; in the end there is surely some mischief. Examine exterior and interior carefully, distinguishing the triple burner; know where [the evil] lodges, adjust in diagnosing, assess the bowels and viscera, and you will see alone as if divine. I record these items for you, to transmit to worthy people.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

The cun is the upper position, the guan the middle position, the chi the lower position.

*Hengquan* is a scale, by which weight can be measured. The *Neijing* says: "Spring corresponds to the compass, summer to the square, autumn to the balance beam, winter to the weight." The nutritive moves within the vessels and the defensive outside them. The nutritive and defensive rise and fall together with the pulse, corresponding to the four seasons without losing their normal measure.

The kidney is northern water, in its prime in winter, and its pulse is sunken. The heart is southern fire, in its prime in summer, and its pulse is surging. The lung is western metal, in its prime in autumn, and its pulse is floating. The liver is eastern wood, in its prime in spring, and its pulse is wiry. These are the constants, without the slightest difference.

The vessels of the human body measure sixteen zhang two chi in total length. With one exhalation the pulse moves three cun; with one inhalation the pulse moves three cun; one exhalation and one inhalation make one breath, in which the pulse moves six cun. In one day and night the water clock falls a hundred marks; a person breathes thirteen thousand five hundred times, and the pulse moves eight hundred and ten zhang, circulating the body fifty times. Thus in one mark a person breathes one hundred thirty-five times and the pulse moves eight zhang one chi; when the water falls two marks, a person breathes two hundred seventy times and the pulse moves sixteen zhang two chi, circulating the body once. The circulation of the vessels ends and begins again, like a ring without end.

The circulation of the vessels begins from the middle burner and pours into the cun kou of the hand taiyin. In two hundred seventy breaths the pulse circulates the body once and returns to the cun kou. The cun kou is the beginning of the vessels' circulation, so vacuity and repletion are diagnosed there. The classic says: "The essentials of vacuity and repletion, death and life, are all seen in the cun kou."

Wind damages yang, so the pulse is floating and vacuous; cold damages yin, so the pulse is firm and hard. What accumulates within is called collected water, so the pulse is sunken and submerged. What branches and scatters outward is called propping rheum, so the pulse is urgent and wiry. Stirred means yin and yang contend, and contention gives rise to pain. Rapid is yang, meaning the evil qi prevails; when yang prevails there are heat and vexation.

When the pulse and disease do not correspond, it is surely caused by transmission and change. The three positions examine the qi of the five viscera; their vacuity and repletion are examined according to position.

Pulses of excess and insufficiency all mean evil qi invading the upright qi. Examine carefully whether it is in the exterior or interior, whether it has entered the bowels or viscera, and treat according to where it lodges.

## Notes

The opening of the chapter "Balancing the Pulse," setting out a general discussion of pulse diagnosis as a dialogue in rhymed four-character verse.

- It outlines the three positions (cun, guan, chi), the seasonal pulses (wiry in spring, surging in summer, floating in autumn, sunken in winter), the visceral pulses (kidney sunken, heart surging, lung floating, liver wiry), the circulation of the pulse around the whole body in a hundred marks of the water clock, and changes of the pulse due to pathogens.
- Cheng Wuji's commentary supplements calculations based on the *Lingshu*, *Nanjing*, etc., such as the length of the vessels (sixteen zhang two chi) and fifty circuits per day.
- Like "Distinguishing the Pulse," "Balancing the Pulse" is held by some to be a later addition by Wang Shuhe or others.
