# §3 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: The superior practitioner knows by observing, the middling practitioner knows by asking, the inferior practitioner knows by the pulse. I wish to hear the explanation. The master said: Someone from the patient's family comes to request [a visit], saying: "The patient suffers from fever and body aches." [Yet] the patient is lying down on their own. The master arrives and examines the pulse: it is sunken and slow; thus one knows the patient has recovered. How is this known? If there were disease in the exterior, the pulse should be floating and large; now the pulse, on the contrary, is sunken and slow, so one knows there is recovery. Suppose the patient says there is sudden pain in the abdomen, yet the patient is sitting up on their own, and the master arrives and takes the pulse: it is floating and large; thus one knows the patient has recovered. How is this known? If there were disease in the interior, the pulse should be sunken and fine; now the pulse is floating and large, so one knows there is recovery.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

Observing reveals the bodily form and signs; asking reveals what one suffers from; the pulse distinguishes exterior and interior. Suffering from fever and body aches means the evil is in the exterior; one should be unable to lie at ease and the pulse should be floating and rapid. Now the patient lies down on their own and the pulse is sunken and slow, meaning the exterior evil has relaxed. There is an interior pulse without exterior signs, so one knows the exterior evil should recover.

Abdominal pain is interior cold; when the pain is severe one cannot get up, and the pulse is sunken and fine. Now the patient sits up on their own and the pulse is floating and large, meaning the interior cold has dissipated. There is an exterior pulse without interior signs, so one knows the interior evil should recover. This is obtained by cross-referencing the three—observing signs, asking about the disease, and palpating the pulse—and one can become a physician who is fully accurate. The *Zhenjing* says: "Knowing one is superior; knowing two is divine; knowing three is divine and enlightened."

## Notes

The clause gives examples of combining observation, inquiry, and pulse diagnosis to judge that a disease is already recovering when the patient's complaints conflict with the actual appearance and pulse.
