# §23 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

At the cun kou, exuberant wei-defensive qi is called "lofty" (lofty: suddenly wild and stout); exuberant ying-nutrient qi is called "brilliant" (brilliant: suddenly moist and lustrous). When lofty and brilliant contend, it is called "rope" (rope: because the sinews of the body are tense and the pulse is stiff and straight). Weak wei-defensive qi is called "timid" (timid: the qi in the heart stirs, pressed and fearful); weak ying-nutrient qi is called "lowly" (lowly: always feeling ashamed in the heart). When timid and lowly contend, it is called "depleted" (depleted: because the five zang organs and six fu organs are all lacking, with qi deficient and feeble). Harmonious wei-defensive qi is called "moderate" (moderate: the four limbs cannot draw themselves in); harmonious ying-nutrient qi is called "slow" (slow: the whole body is heavy and one only wishes to sleep). When slow and moderate contend, it is called "deep" (deep: the lumbus is stiff, there is urgent pain in the abdomen, and one only wishes to lie down and does not wish to walk).

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

Lofty means suddenly wild and stout. The *Neijing* says: "When yin cannot overcome its yang, the flow of the pulse becomes pressing and fast, and [yang] combines to produce mania." The wei-defense is yang qi; exuberant wei-defense with sudden wildness means yin cannot overcome yang. The *Zhenjing* says: "The wei-defensive qi warms the parting flesh, fills the skin and hair, fattens the interstices, and governs opening and closing." Exuberant wei-defensive qi producing stoutness means qi is exuberant outside.

Brilliant means suddenly moist and lustrous. The ying-nutrient is blood, which makes the body flourish; the ying-nutrient is exuberant, so the body is suddenly lustrous and moist.

Rope means the sinews of the body are tense and the pulse straight. When both ying-nutrient and wei-defense are exuberant, the sinews and collaterals are full and tense.

Timid means the qi in the heart stirs, pressed and fearful. The wei-defense emerges from the upper jiao; when it is weak, the upper part is deficient and the qi in the heart stirs, pressed and fearful.

Lowly means always feeling ashamed in the heart. The *Zhenjing* says: "Blood is the spirit qi." When blood is weak the spirit is weak, so one always feels ashamed.

Depleted means deficiency and feebleness of the five zang organs and six fu organs. The wei-defense protects yang; the ying-nutrient nourishes yin. When both are deficient, the five zang organs and six fu organs lose their nourishment and all become lacking with deficient qi.

Moderate means the four limbs cannot draw themselves in. When the wei-defensive qi alone is harmonious and not in accord with the ying-nutrient qi, the ying-nutrient is diseased. The *Neijing* says: "The eyes receive blood and can see; the feet receive blood and can walk; the palms receive blood and can grasp; the fingers receive blood and can pinch." The four limbs not drawing in is because the ying-nutrient blood is diseased and cannot irrigate and nourish.

Slow means the body is heavy and one only wishes to sleep. When the ying-nutrient qi alone is harmonious and not in accord with the wei-defensive qi, the wei-defense is diseased. A heavy body with eyes wishing to sleep means the wei-defense is diseased and qi does not spread.

[The ying-nutrient qi alone] is harmonious within and the wei-defensive qi alone harmonious without; the ying-nutrient and wei-defense are not in accord with each other, and they contend to produce disease. Stiffness of the lumbus means the wei-defense is not free outside; pain in the abdomen means the ying-nutrient is not harmonious inside; only wishing to lie down and not to walk means the ying-nutrient and wei-defense do not circulate. ## Notes

The clause organizes the exuberance and weakness of wei-defense and ying-nutrient qi appearing in the cun kou pulse, and their combinations, under the names lofty, brilliant, rope; timid, lowly, depleted; moderate, slow, deep; and explains the bodily and mental signs of each in small-character notes. The parenthetical text is the interlinear (small-character) note of the main text, which Cheng Wuji's commentary repeats and explains.
