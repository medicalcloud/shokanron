# §23 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

At the cun kou, exuberant defensive qi is called "lofty" (lofty: suddenly wild and stout); exuberant nutritive qi is called "brilliant" (brilliant: suddenly moist and lustrous). When lofty and brilliant contend, it is called "rope" (rope: because the sinews of the body are tense and the pulse is stiff and straight). Weak defensive qi is called "timid" (timid: the qi in the heart stirs, pressed and fearful); weak nutritive qi is called "lowly" (lowly: always feeling ashamed in the heart). When timid and lowly contend, it is called "depleted" (depleted: because the five viscera and six bowels are all lacking, with qi vacuous and feeble). Harmonious defensive qi is called "moderate" (moderate: the four limbs cannot draw themselves in); harmonious nutritive qi is called "slow" (slow: the whole body is heavy and one only wishes to sleep). When slow and moderate contend, it is called "sunken" (sunken: the lumbus is stiff, there is urgent pain in the abdomen, and one only wishes to lie down and does not wish to walk).

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

Lofty means suddenly wild and stout. The *Neijing* says: "When yin cannot overcome its yang, the flow of the pulse becomes pressing and fast, and [yang] combines to produce mania." The defensive is yang qi; exuberant defensive with sudden wildness means yin cannot overcome yang. The *Zhenjing* says: "The defensive qi warms the parting flesh, fills the skin and hair, fattens the interstices, and governs opening and closing." Exuberant defensive qi producing stoutness means qi is exuberant outside.

Brilliant means suddenly moist and lustrous. The nutritive is blood, which makes the body flourish; the nutritive is exuberant, so the body is suddenly lustrous and moist.

Rope means the sinews of the body are tense and the pulse straight. When both nutritive and defensive are exuberant, the sinews and network vessels are full and tense.

Timid means the qi in the heart stirs, pressed and fearful. The defensive emerges from the upper burner; when it is weak, the upper part is vacuous and the qi in the heart stirs, pressed and fearful.

Lowly means always feeling ashamed in the heart. The *Zhenjing* says: "Blood is the spirit qi." When blood is weak the spirit is weak, so one always feels ashamed.

Depleted means vacuity and feebleness of the five viscera and six bowels. The defensive protects yang; the nutritive nourishes yin. When both are vacuous, the five viscera and six bowels lose their nourishment and all become lacking with vacuous qi.

Moderate means the four limbs cannot draw themselves in. When the defensive qi alone is harmonious and not in accord with the nutritive qi, the nutritive is diseased. The *Neijing* says: "The eyes receive blood and can see; the feet receive blood and can walk; the palms receive blood and can grasp; the fingers receive blood and can pinch." The four limbs not drawing in is because the nutritive blood is diseased and cannot irrigate and nourish.

Slow means the body is heavy and one only wishes to sleep. When the nutritive qi alone is harmonious and not in accord with the defensive qi, the defensive is diseased. A heavy body with eyes wishing to sleep means the defensive is diseased and qi does not spread.

[The nutritive qi alone] is harmonious within and the defensive qi alone harmonious without; the nutritive and defensive are not in accord with each other, and they contend to produce disease. Stiffness of the lumbus means the defensive is not free outside; pain in the abdomen means the nutritive is not harmonious inside; only wishing to lie down and not to walk means the nutritive and defensive do not circulate. ## Notes

The clause organizes the exuberance and weakness of defensive and nutritive qi appearing in the cun kou pulse, and their combinations, under the names lofty, brilliant, rope; timid, lowly, depleted; moderate, slow, sunken; and explains the bodily and mental signs of each in small-character notes. The parenthetical text is the interlinear (small-character) note of the main text, which Cheng Wuji's commentary repeats and explains.
