# §21 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Question: "Gathering, then suddenly sinking" is called slippery—what does this mean? The master said: Sunken is pure yin; gathering is upright yang. Yin and yang unite harmoniously, making the pulse slippery, and the guan and chi are spontaneously even. When the yangming pulse is slightly sunken, eating and drinking are spontaneously possible. When the shaoyin pulse is slightly slippery—slippery being the name for tight floating—this is yin repletion; the person will certainly sweat on the inner thighs and be damp below the genitals.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

The pulse coming large and exuberant, gathering and sinking, is called "gathering, then suddenly sinking," exactly like a rolling pearl. Sunken is visceral qi, so it is called pure yin; gathering is bowel qi, so it is called upright yang. Slippery means the qi of yin and yang does not prevail one-sidedly. The guan and chi are spontaneously even. The yangming pulse being slightly sunken means a yin pulse appears in a yang position, so yin prevails one-sidedly and yang is insufficient. Yangming is the stomach pulse; there is much yin in the stomach, so eating and drinking are spontaneously possible. The shaoyin pulse being slightly slippery means a yang pulse appears in a yin position, so yang prevails one-sidedly and yin is insufficient; because yang converges on the yin region, it is called yin repletion. The thighs and genitals are the region of the shaoyin; now yang heat converges on the yin and will certainly fume and effuse the fluids, draining them outward, so there is sweating on the inner thighs and dampness below the genitals.

## Notes

The clause defines the "slippery pulse" as a fluent pulse like a rolling pearl, in which yin (sunken) and yang (gathering) unite harmoniously, and goes on to changes in the fuyang (yangming) and shaoyin pulses. Much of the passage is difficult, and interpretations of phrases such as "slippery being the name for tight floating" vary.
