# 刻傷寒論序（江瓘） (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Preface to the Printed *Shanghan lun*

Composed by Jiang Guan, Huangnan of Xin'an.

The preface says: Since the learning of Xuan[yuan] and Qi[bo] ceased to be transmitted, there remain only the two books *Suwen* and *Nanjing*, and these too have many errors and gaps; their surviving texts and profound meanings have had few in each generation who investigated them well. Later students, ignorant of the original texts, applied doubtful things by their own judgment, and the classics fell into confusion. With Mr. Zhang of Changsha of the Later Han, for the first time, based on the "Treatise on Heat" of the *Suwen*, Yi Yin's decoctions were expanded, discussions freely composed, doubtful meanings brought out, and the classics were once again made clear. Then it was compiled by Wang Shuhe and annotated by Cheng Wuji. Afterward the school of Pang [Anshi], Zhu [Gong], Han [Zhihe], and Xu [Shuwei] also each made contributions. Lifting the guideline and holding up the essentials, nothing goes beyond the four methods of vomiting, sweating, purgation, and warming. Indeed, one pattern, one formula—selected ten thousand times and hitting ten thousand times—turning back death and raising life, matching like tallies across a thousand years. Tao Yinju called it the ancestor of all formulas; Sun the Perfected Man marveled at its singular divine efficacy—how could this be without evidence? Yet different lands suit different things, and antiquity and the present differ in their circulations; yin and yang, vacuity and repletion intertwine, and their signs are extremely subtle; sweating and vomiting or purgation are opposites, and their calamities extremely swift. Add to this that mediocre practitioners cling stubbornly, confused and mistaken without reflection, turning slight ailments into the transformation of incurable illness and sunken chronic diseases into ones beyond hope of revival—there is a reason for this. On the whole, this book's ordering is clear, but its wording is elegant and profound, hard for the common folk to enter; how many have discerning eyes? Thus in medical circles few read it and sellers cannot sell it. Moreover, Zhang's classic and Wang's transmission are often reversed in order, copying errors are mixed in, and the printed editions are blurred and incomplete; lovers of antiquity regret this. Alas! When the *Maijue* appeared, the *Maijing* was hidden; when the *Baiwen* prevailed, the *Shanghan lun* was departed from. Compared to vulgar Confucians who recite only examination essays and are ignorant of the classics and commentaries, the fault is the same. Mr. Wang Xishuo, renowned for broad elegance, lamented the confusion of vulgar learning and pitied the early deaths of the people; he brought out the good edition stored in his family, showed it to Mr. Wang Chujing to collate repeatedly, then ordered it printed and asked me for a preface. Being often ill, I have loved medicine but have not mastered it; yet I have savored Zhongjing's treatise for years. So, drawing on the past to illuminate the present, I trace its stream from source to end after the scrolls, and also commend the two gentlemen's service to Changsha. If students truly immerse themselves in this book and seek where it leads, they can surpass the worthies of the past and bring benevolence and longevity to the present age.

An auspicious day in the *yisi* year of Jiajing.

## Notes

The preface written by Jiang Guan of the Ming (known as the compiler of the *Mingyi lei'an*) for the publication of Cheng Wuji's annotated edition (*yisi* year of Jiajing = 1545). In Kanripo it is placed at the end of the book, but it is a different text from the Song edition's "Afterword" (Chapter 26).

- It surveys the transmission of the *Shanghan lun* as Zhang Zhongjing → Wang Shuhe → Cheng Wuji → the Song-period Pang Anshi, Zhu Gong, Han Zhihe, and Xu Shuwei, and summarizes the essentials of treatment as the four methods of vomiting, sweating, purgation, and warming.
- The phrase "yin and yang, vacuity and repletion intertwine, and their signs are extremely subtle; sweating and vomiting or purgation are opposites, and their calamities extremely swift" draws on paragraph 28 of the "Examples of Cold Damage" chapter.
- It laments that the popularity of the *Maijue* (regarded as falsely attributed, by Gao Yangsheng) and the *Shanghan baiwen* caused the original classics to be neglected, and praises the good edition collated and printed by Wang Xishuo and Wang Chujing.
