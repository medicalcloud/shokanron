# 註解傷寒論序（嚴器之） (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Preface to the Annotated *Shanghan lun*

When earlier sages created, there were always later ones who continued and transmitted, so that their teachings could become manifest in the world. The way of medicine originated with Yandi and Huangdi; through supremely divine subtlety the classical formulas first arose. Then Yi Yin, with the talent of a primordial sage, compiled decoctions, causing the illnesses of the common people all to be removed and the living beings of ten thousand generations universally to receive rescue. Zhang Zhongjing of the Later Han further expanded decoctions into the *Shanghan zubing lun* in some ten-odd scrolls, and afterward medical formulas were greatly complete. Here the earlier and later sages matched like tallies. In the Jin, the Imperial Physician Wang Shuhe compiled and ordered Zhongjing's book, making it a complete set. People of old regarded Zhongjing's corpus of formulas as the ancestor of all formulas, because it well continued and transmitted what the earlier sages created. That it has not fallen to the ground for more than a thousand years to the present is also due to Mr. Wang's work of elucidation. The *Shanghan lun* in ten scrolls is refined and profound in its words and concise yet detailed in its methods, not something the narrowly informed and shallow can fathom. Though later there were students of it, each merely founded his own school, and no elucidation was seen. I have humbly practiced medicine, and from youth to old age have savored Zhongjing's book for more than fifty years. Although I roughly gained its gate and nearly ascended to its hall, I had not yet entered its chamber, and was always dissatisfied. Recently I happened to meet Mr. Cheng of Liaoshe, whose discussions are broad and learned, whose art is thorough, and who has family learning. He annotated the *Shanghan* in ten scrolls and brought it out to show me. Within its 397 methods, he analyzes similarities and differences, clarifies hidden mysteries, sets out the principles of the pulse, distinguishes yin and yang, makes exterior and interior manifest, and makes sweating and purgation clearly visible. After the 112 formulas, he clarifies the origins of their names, reveals the masters among the medicinal natures, where the ten preparations divide in lightness and heaviness, where the methods of the seven relations are seen, distinguishes what suits each qi and flavor, and clarifies where supplementing and draining are appropriate. And throughout he quotes the *Neijing* and draws on many other views, so that none of his discussions of formulas and methods is not apt. Truly it is what earlier worthies had not said and later students had not known; he has grasped Zhongjing's deep meaning. What I was formerly dissatisfied with, I have now fully reached in its depths. Having seen the book in person, it is truly hard to remain silent; not minding my crude writing, I briefly preface it. Written on the mid-autumn day of the *jiazi* year by Yan Qizhi of Luoyang.

## Notes

The preface contributed by Yan Qizhi, a contemporary of Cheng Wuji, to Cheng Wuji's *Zhujie Shanghan lun*. The *jiazi* year is said to be the 4th year of Huangtong of the Jin (1144).

- It describes the medical tradition as the lineage Yandi and Huangdi → Yi Yin → Zhang Zhongjing → Wang Shuhe (a framework shared with the Song officials' preface).
- It introduces Cheng Wuji as a physician of Liaoshe (near present-day Liaocheng, Shandong) with family learning.
- As features of Cheng Wuji's commentary it names the analysis of the 397 methods and the explanation of the origin of the names, medicinal natures, qi and flavors, and supplementing and draining of the 112 formulas, with quotations from the *Neijing*. That Cheng Wuji's commentary records the nature and flavor after each formula and explains its composition by quoting the *Neijing* is as seen throughout the translation of this work.
