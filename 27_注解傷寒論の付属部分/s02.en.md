# 新刻傷寒論序（鄭佐） (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Preface to the Newly Printed *Shanghan lun*

The *Shanghan lun* is concise and strict in language and profound in its meaning. It divides into the six channels, and its methods are now detailed, now brief. Where detailed, the principles and examples are clearly distinguished, not superfluous; where brief, the purport is all-embracing, not omitted. Scattered, it falls cleanly into distinct categories; fused, it is bound together into one thread. It depends only on how those who read and use it fare. Confucian scholars have no time to read it, and few physicians can read it; thus its subtle words and essential meanings remain hidden and unrevealed, until some say "this is not a complete book," and set about dividing it into categories and laying it out flat, continuing with conjectures as novelties, mixing in many formulas and calling it complete—like smelting ore and fine gold together, or making one robe of sable and dog fur. Books such as the *Huoren shu* and *Shache [chuifa]* are all pests of Zhongjing. As I see Mr. Cheng's commentary, he alone was able to investigate the bequeathed classic thoroughly, staying with it from beginning to end and elucidating much. Though at times he interprets in order following the text, like transmitting a general's orders to the three armies, he does not dare to add or alter recklessly; the hearer, merely carrying it out with care, will gain for himself. His merit regarding this book is not shallow. Yet the world has those who abandon the voice and chase only the echo, and so the treatise and the commentary were buried together—a pity. If physicians carry on like this, there is no hope that they will produce wonders that accord with the marvelous use of a thousand years ago. Of those unfortunate enough to get this disease, how many can escape slaughter by physicians at the throat? Mr. Wang Chujing, a man of my village, grieved over this; he strove to purchase good editions, collated them repeatedly, and, fearing that it would not be transmitted far, had it carved on blocks for the public. Ah! The medical *Suwen* and *Lingshu* compare with the Confucian Six Classics; the *Shanghan lun* may be compared with the *Analects* and *Mencius*. The Six Classics, *Analects*, and *Mencius* all survive, yet without reading them one cannot understand; and yet those charged with keeping the people alive sometimes abandon them and bear seeing the ditches and gullies fill [with the dead]. As for this book, it is already rarely seen in the world, and reading it through and mastering it is not easy; how much more for one who, without being charged with keeping people alive, strives out of fondness to stop the wrongful deaths at the hands of physicians. How far apart are these two uses of the mind! I am therefore privately moved, and write this preface.

On the full moon of the sixth month of summer, the twenty-fourth year of Jiajing, the year being *yisi*, at Yanzhen in She,

written by Zheng Zuo of Lübin.

## Notes

The preface written by Zheng Zuo in the 24th year of Jiajing of the Ming (1545) for Wang Chujing's (Wang Jichuan's) publication of Cheng Wuji's annotated edition, the *Zhujie Shanghan lun*.

- Likening the *Shanghan lun* to the Confucian *Analects* and *Mencius*, it emphasizes the deep meaning within its concision.
- It criticizes later books (such as the *Huoren shu*) that reclassified the *Shanghan lun* and added their own theories, and highly praises Cheng Wuji's commentary for its fidelity to the classic text.
- "Shache" in "活人殺車等書" is thought to refer to the *Shache chuifa* (a book by Tao Hua).
