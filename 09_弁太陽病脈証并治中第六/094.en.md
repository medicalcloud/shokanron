# Clause 94 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Taiyang disease not yet resolved: when the pulse is still (*ting*) at both yin and yang (one version writes "faint"), there will certainly first be shivering, and with sweating it resolves. When only the yang pulse is faint, sweat comes out first and it resolves. When only the yin pulse is faint (one version writes "the chi pulse is replete"), purgation resolves it. If one wishes to purge, Tiaowei Chengqi tang is appropriate. 46. Use the thirty-third formula above. (One version says to use Da Chaihu tang.)

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

A pulse still at both yin and yang, with neither prevailing, means the qi of yin and yang is harmonized. The Classic says: "When at the three places cunkou, guanshang, and chizhong the pulse is equal in size, depth, and rate, yin and yang are balanced, and even if [the disease] is severe it should be cured." Here yin and yang are already harmonized, so there will certainly first be shivering, and with sweating it resolves. When only the yang pulse is faint, yang is insufficient and yin in surplus; the Classic says, "When yang is vacuous and yin exuberant, sweating cures." When the yin pulse is faint, yin is insufficient and yang in surplus; the Classic says, "When yang is exuberant and yin vacuous, purgation cures."

## Notes

A clause judging from the pulse whether the disease will resolve by sweating or by purgation.

- "The pulse is still at both yin and yang" is hard to interpret: one view takes it as cun and chi pulses being balanced (Cheng Wuji), another as the pulse temporarily sinking and becoming hard to feel. In the latter view it is a sign of "shiver-sweating" (shivering, then sweating and recovery).
- If the yang (cun) pulse is faint, it resolves by sweating; if the yin (chi) pulse is faint, by purgation.
