# Clause 1 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

The disease of Taiyang: the pulse is floating, the head and nape are stiff and painful, and there is aversion to cold.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

The Classic (the "Shanghan li" chapter) says: "When the chi and cun pulses are both floating, the Taiyang has contracted the disease." When the Taiyang contracts the disease—since the Taiyang governs the exterior and governs the qi of all the yang [meridians]—a floating pulse, a stiff and painful head and nape, and aversion to cold indicate a disease of the Taiyang exterior.

## Notes

This clause is traditionally regarded as the outline (*tigang*) of Taiyang disease. A floating pulse, a stiff and painful head and nape, and aversion to cold are taken as its defining signs, and the following Taiyang clauses are commonly read with this definition in mind.

- Floating pulse: generally understood to show that the disease is in the exterior (the body surface).
- Stiff and painful head and nape: often explained by the course of the Taiyang meridian (the foot Taiyang bladder meridian), which runs from the head through the nape and back.
- Aversion to cold: feeling chilled. On why fever is not mentioned here, one common explanation is that fever does not always appear at the onset (compare Clause 3: "whether fever has already appeared or not, there must be aversion to cold").

Cheng Wuji cites the "Shanghan li" chapter ("when the chi and cun pulses are both floating, the Taiyang has contracted the disease") and explains, in terms of pulses and meridians, why this is a disease of the exterior.
