# Clause 12 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

In taiyang wind-strike (*zhongfeng*), the yang [pulse] is floating and the yin [pulse] is weak. When the yang [pulse] is floating, heat arises of itself; when the yin [pulse] is weak, sweat comes out of itself. When there is huddled aversion to cold, shivering aversion to wind, feather-warm fever, noisy nose, and dry retching, Guizhi tang (Cinnamon Twig Decoction) governs it. Formula 1.

Guizhi (cinnamon twig) 3 liang (remove the bark); shaoyao (peony) 3 liang; gancao (licorice) 2 liang (mix-fried); shengjiang (fresh ginger) 3 liang (sliced); dazao (jujube) 12 pieces (split).

Of the five ingredients above, pound three. Use 7 sheng of water, boil over a gentle fire down to 3 sheng, remove the dregs, and when it is suitably warm take 1 sheng. A short while after taking it, sip more than 1 sheng of hot thin porridge to assist the power of the medicine. Keep [the patient] warmly covered for about one *shi* (two hours); it is best if the whole body becomes moist with a slight sweat. Do not let [sweat] pour out like flowing water, [or] the disease will certainly not be removed. If sweat appears and the disease is cured after one dose, stop the later doses; it is not necessary to finish the preparation. If there is no sweating, take another dose in the same way. If there is still no sweating, shorten the interval for the later doses a little, so that the three doses are finished within about half a day. If the disease is severe, take it through one day and one night, and observe [the patient] for a full day. If the signs of the disease are still present after one preparation has been finished, make it again and take it. If sweat still does not come out, take up to two or three preparations. Avoid raw and cold foods, sticky and slippery foods, meat and noodles, the five pungent vegetables, alcohol and dairy, and foul-smelling things.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

The yang [pulse] is used to observe the defensive (*wei*) [qi], and the yin [pulse] to observe the nutritive (*ying*) [qi]. A floating yang pulse means the defensive [qi] has been struck by wind; a weak yin pulse means the nutritive qi is weak. When wind joins the defensive [qi], the defensive becomes replete and the nutritive becomes vacuous; therefore there is fever and sweat comes out of itself. This is what the Classic means by "taiyang disease with fever and sweating: this is weak nutritive and strong defensive." *Seise* (huddled) means insufficiency; it describes aversion to cold. *Xixi* (shivering) means a chilly shudder; it describes aversion to wind. When the defensive is vacuous there is aversion to wind; when the nutritive is vacuous there is aversion to cold. That there is aversion to cold and also aversion to wind although the nutritive is weak and the defensive strong is because spontaneous sweating slackens the skin and loosens the interstices (*couli*), which also produces aversion to wind. *Xixi* (feather-warm) means a glowing heat, as if covered by folded wings; it indicates heat in the exterior. Noisy nose and dry retching are due to wind obstructing and qi counterflowing. Guizhi tang is given to harmonize the nutritive and defensive and to disperse the wind evil.

(Nature and flavor noted in the interlinear notes on the ingredients) Guizhi: acrid, hot. Shaoyao: bitter and sour, slightly cold. Gancao: sweet, neutral. Shengjiang: acrid, warm. Dazao: sweet, warm.

The *Neijing* says: "Acrid and sweet disperse and are yang." Guizhi tang is an acrid-sweet preparation, and thus it disperses wind evil. The *Neijing* says: "Where wind excess prevails, calm it with acrid, assist with bitter and sweet, moderate it with sweet, and contract it with sour." Therefore guizhi is the chief, and shaoyao and gancao are the assistants. The *Neijing* says: "When wind excess is within, moderate it with sweet and disperse it with acrid." Therefore shengjiang and dazao are the envoys.

## Notes

This clause describes the typical signs of taiyang wind-strike (Clause 2: fever, sweating, aversion to wind, moderate pulse) and its principal formula, Guizhi tang. Guizhi tang is the first formula of the *Shanghan lun* and the basis of many later formulas.

- "The yang is floating and the yin is weak": one reading takes this as a pulse that is floating at light pressure and weak at heavy pressure; another as a floating cun pulse and a weak chi pulse. Cheng Wuji equates yang and yin with the defensive and nutritive qi and explains it as "weak nutritive and strong defensive" (Clause 95).
- *Seise*, *xixi* and *xixi* are taken as onomatopoeic/mimetic words for chills, sensitivity to wind, and the quality of the fever.
- "Pound three [of the five ingredients]": usually read as pounding the three other than jujube and fresh ginger.
- Administration: hot porridge is sipped to assist the medicine, and the patient is covered warmly to induce a slight, moist sweat over the whole body. The text gives detailed instructions—do not induce profuse sweating, stop once sweating occurs, and shorten the intervals if it does not—and these are regarded as basic precautions for the sweating method.
- The porridge, warm covering, and dietary prohibitions are frequently cited in later literature as the standard way of taking Guizhi tang.
