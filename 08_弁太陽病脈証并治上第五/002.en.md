# Clause 2 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Taiyang disease with fever, sweating, aversion to wind, and a moderate pulse is called wind attack (*zhongfeng*).

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

Wind is yang; cold is yin. Wind damages the wei-defense (*wei*) [qi]. Fever, sweating, and aversion to wind mean that the wei-defense [qi] has been struck by wind. When the ying-nutrient (*ying*) [qi] is diseased, there is fever without sweating, and aversion to cold rather than to wind. When the wei-defense [qi] is diseased, there is fever with sweating, and aversion to wind rather than to cold. Since the wei-defense is yang and guards the exterior, when it is diseased it cannot guard and secure the exterior, the skin and interstices become loose, and so there is sweating and aversion to wind. The pulse is tight in cold damage and moderate in wind damage because the nature of cold is tense and urgent, while the nature of wind is slack and loose.

## Notes

The definition of "wind attack" within Taiyang disease: in addition to the Taiyang signs of Clause 1, there are fever, sweating, aversion to wind, and a moderate pulse. It forms a pair with "cold damage" in Clause 3 (no sweating, tight pulse).

- *Zhongfeng* here is an externally contracted disease caused by being "struck" by wind; it is different from the later use of the same term for stroke.
- Sweating and the moderate pulse are regarded as the points that distinguish it from cold damage.
- Cheng Wuji explains wind attack and cold damage with the idea that wind damages the wei-defensive qi (the yang qi guarding the surface) and cold damages the ying-nutrient qi (the yin blood within the vessels).
