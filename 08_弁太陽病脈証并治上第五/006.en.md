# Clause 6 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Taiyang disease with fever and thirst but without aversion to cold is warm disease (*wenbing*). If, after sweating has been induced, the body is scorchingly hot, it is called wind-warmth (*fengwen*). In the disease of wind-warmth, the pulse is floating at both yin and yang; there is spontaneous sweating, heaviness of the body, much sleeping, the breath through the nose is certainly snoring, and speech comes out with difficulty. If purgation has been applied, there are inhibited urination, a fixed stare, and incontinence. If fire [therapy] has been applied, there is a slight yellow coloring, and in severe cases [the patient] is as if in fright epilepsy, with occasional tugging and slackening (convulsions). If [the patient] is fumigated with fire, one mistake still prolongs the days, but a second mistake hastens the time of death.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

Fever and thirst without aversion to cold belong to the yangming. Here the taiyang has received the evil, but one knows that it is warm disease, not cold damage. Warmth accumulates and becomes heat; therefore there are fever and thirst without aversion to cold.

In cold damage, the body becomes cool once sweating has been completed. If the body is still scorchingly hot after sweating has been completed, it is not cold damage but wind-warmth. Wind damages the upper part, and the yang receives the wind qi. When wind and warmth combine, they damage the defensive [qi]. A pulse floating at both yin and yang with spontaneous sweating means that the defensive [qi] has received the evil. The defensive is qi. Wind damages the defensive; warmth damages the qi. Heaviness of the body and much sleeping mean that the defensive has received wind-warmth and the qi has become clouded. Snoring breath and difficulty in speaking mean that wind-warmth is severe on the outside and the qi is blocked and does not flow. If purgation is applied, it damages the visceral qi. The taiyang is the bladder channel. The *Neijing* says: "When the bladder is inhibited there is dribbling block; when it does not retain there is enuresis." Dribbling block means inhibited urination. The taiyang vessel arises from the inner canthus. The *Neijing* says: "Upturned pupils mean the taiyang is insufficient; upturned eyes mean the taiyang is already cut off." Inhibited urination, a fixed stare, and incontinence are due to the exhaustion of fluids and damage to the visceral qi after purgation, while wind-warmth prevails outside; this is what the Classic calls "about to be cut off," and it is difficult to treat. If fire is applied, the fire assists the wind-warmth to become heat. In mild cases the heat stagnates and yellowing appears; in severe cases the heat is extreme and generates wind, as in fright epilepsy, with occasional convulsions. Having been treated with fire first is the first mistake; fumigating with fire again is the second mistake. One mistake merely prolongs the days without cure, but a second mistake surely leads to danger; hence "hastens the time of death."

## Notes

This clause describes "warm disease," which resembles taiyang disease but differs from cold damage and wind-strike; "wind-warmth," which follows mistaken sweating; and further deterioration from mistaken treatment (purgation, fire therapy).

- Warm disease: fever with thirst and no aversion to cold distinguish it from cold damage and wind-strike. The later school of warm diseases (*wenbing xue*) regards this clause as one of its sources.
- Wind-warmth: often understood as the state in which heat has intensified after acrid-warm sweating was applied to warm disease. Some hold that "wind-warmth" here differs from the later warm-disease term of the same name (an externally contracted heat disease of spring).
- "One mistake still prolongs the days, but a second mistake hastens the time of death" is often cited as a warning against repeated mistreatment.
