# Clause 178 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

A pulse that, on pressure, comes moderately, stops once at times, and comes again is called bound. Further, a pulse that comes stirring and stops midway, then comes a little rapid, with something returning midway and, on the contrary, stirring, is called bound; it is yin. A pulse that comes stirring and stops midway, cannot return by itself, and then stirs again is called intermittent; it is yin. One who has these pulses is certainly difficult to treat.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

Of the bound and intermittent pulses, one is due to evil qi lodging and binding, the other to vacuity and debility of the true qi. When the pulse comes stirring and stops midway, if it can return by itself and then comes a little rapid, the stopping is due to evil qi lodging and binding, and it is called bound yin. If it stirs, stops midway, cannot return by itself, and stirs again only as yin and yang draw on each other through respiration, the true qi is at the extreme of debility; it is called intermittent yin, a pulse difficult to treat. The Classic says, "With a bound pulse one lives; with an intermittent pulse one dies"—this is what is meant.

## Notes

A clause defining the bound and intermittent pulses of the previous clause. Both stop midway; the bound pulse returns by itself and continues after stopping, while the intermittent pulse cannot return by itself and beats again after a pause. The intermittent pulse is regarded as showing debility of the visceral qi with poor prognosis. It is the last clause of the lower taiyang chapter.
