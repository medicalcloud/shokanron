# §5 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

The pulse is soft and weak: weak, on the contrary, at the guan; soft, on the contrary, at the top; feeble, on the contrary, above; hesitant, on the contrary, below. Feeble means yang qi is insufficient; hesitant means there is no blood. When yang qi is, on the contrary, feeble, one is struck by wind, sweats, and, on the contrary, is agitated and vexed. Hesitant means there is no blood, with cold limbs and cold. If sweating is induced when yang is feeble, there is agitation with inability to sleep.

## Translation of Cheng Wuji's commentary

The cun and guan are yang, and the pulse should be floating and exuberant. Weak, on the contrary, at the guan means the interior qi falls short; soft, on the contrary, at the top means the exterior qi falls short. The wei-defense moves outside the vessels; floating is above and examines the wei-defense; feeble, on the contrary, above means yang qi is insufficient. The ying-nutrient moves within the vessels; deep is below and examines the ying-nutrient; hesitant, on the contrary, below means there is no blood. When yang is feeble and cannot secure the outside, the interstices open and become loose, and wind lodges there, causing sweating with restlessness. Without blood, yin is deficient and does not connect smoothly with yang, so there are cold limbs and cold. When yang is feeble and there are no fluids, one cannot sweat; if sweating is induced, yang will surely collapse and there will be agitation. The classic says: "Profuse sweating collapses yang, leading to deficiency, aversion to wind, and restlessness with inability to sleep."

## Notes

The clause judges insufficiency of yang qi and of blood from the pulse quality at each position, and prohibits sweating, since sweating such a person causes agitation and sleeplessness. It is not found in the three yin and three yang chapters and is one of the clauses unique to the "may / may not" chapters.
