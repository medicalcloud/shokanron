# §1 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

In the yiwei year, a pestilence broke out greatly in my town, and of the servants in my household about six or seven in ten took to their beds. Mr. Shen Nanfang (Mingqing), the He and Huan (famous ancient physicians) of our Wu region, was in Haiyu (Changshu), and by his power nearly all who were near death were raised up; my household received a great kindness from Mr. Shen. Not knowing what art Mr. Shen used to be so godlike, I asked him. He said: "How could I have searched the secret classics of the dragon treasury and opened the profound teachings of the blue bag to be so godlike? I have merely glimpsed a spot or two in Zhongjing's *Shanghan lun*." I said: "I have long heard of this book from my father, but it is utterly unobtainable in the bookshops." He said: "I do indeed have it." When I read it, I knew it to be the book annotated by Cheng Wuji. Yet its copying errors could not be corrected and its punctuation could not be parsed. Then I bought several copies, used them to correct the characters and parse the sentences, filled in the omissions, and emended the errors. Mr. Shen said: "This may be called a complete book; [you are] a loyal minister of Zhongjing." I declined as incompetent. My late father charged me: "Have it printed and thereby benefit our compatriots." I, the unworthy orphan, said: "Yes, yes." Mr. Shen said: "The *Jingui Yaolüe* is Zhongjing's secret for treating miscellaneous diseases; why not print it together, to show the ancients' methods of attacking, supplementing and draining, and moderating and adjusting?" My late father said: "Young man, remember this." I, the unworthy orphan, said: "Respectfully [I will]. Since they are printed together, what name shall it take?" My late father said: "Very well—name it *Zhongjing quanshu* (Complete Works of Zhongjing)." After the printing was finished, I further obtained a Song-edition *Shanghan lun*. I had long known that Cheng's annotated edition was not the complete text, and on obtaining this book it was more precious to me than a jade disk; turning its scrolls, I came to know how careless Cheng's was, and so I printed it together as well—was this not to carry on my late father's wish? Also, among old papers I found the *Shanghan leizheng* in three scrolls, which summarizes Zhongjing's book, removing its complexity to return it to simplicity, gathering its scattered parts into one; on diseases, signs, pulses, and formulas it is as clear and complete as a finger pointing at the moon, and Zhongjing's methods are thereby brilliantly displayed without omission; so I appended it at the end. I lament, therefore, that people of the world have hitherto died without living out their allotted span. Zhongjing exhausted his mind on Xuan[yuan] and Qi[bo], distinguished signs to the breadth of a hair, and composed one hundred and twelve formulas to preserve the lives of the people—how benevolent and loving this is, raising a whole age to the realm of benevolence and longevity! Yet today's practitioners of medicine abandon the root and chase the branches; the better ones say "Dongyuan," the narrower ones say "Danxi," and that is all. Those reputed to have the highest understanding take the *Yuji weiyi* as their authority, while as for the *Suwen*, the *Lingshu*, or the *Xuanzhu miyu*, they are blank and bewildered and do not know their purport. Speak to them of Zhang Zhongjing or Liu Hejian and they hardly know who these men were or when they lived, yet they shamelessly say: "That I can cure disease is enough; why strive for the lofty and remote?" Moreover, of those today who read the books of Xuan[yuan] and Qi[bo], they surely sneer: "This fellow merely reads his father's books and does not know the changes of war." Now, people who do not know change certainly exist in the world; but to abandon [the classics] because their changes are hard to master is like choking while eating and then giving up food in seeking to nourish life. Surely it is not so. Then how do I know that the printing of this book today will not be greatly ridiculed by the meat-eaters (those in office)? Someone said: "Lu Xuangong, when in office, healed the realm with his memorials, and when in straits, collected formula books to heal the multitudes; you too surely have a serene concern for the world." I said: "No, no; this is my late father's wish. My late father once, by his memorials, sought to heal the relation of father and son, to heal the spread of factions, to heal the sufferings of the people of the southeast, and by forthright remonstrance to heal the incurable ills of the flatterers; so his days of stumbling were many and his days in office few. And this printing—was it not my late father's wish, the same as Xuangong's? Now my late father has been dead nearly four years and the book is complete. My late father's heart of worry when retired among rivers and lakes was, I think, one and the same with his heart of worry when advancing in the court—equally boundless." A guest said: "You actually did this, yet you attribute it to your late father's wish—is this not what is called crediting one's parents with what is good?" I, the unworthy orphan, said: "No, no; this is my late father's wish."

## Notes

The preface in which Zhao Kaimei of the Ming (sobriquet Qingchang Daoren) records how the *Zhongjing quanshu* came to be published. There is no Cheng Wuji commentary.

- In the yiwei year (1595), an epidemic in Changshu was treated by the physician Shen Nanfang using the *Shanghan lun* (Cheng Wuji's annotated edition), which led Zhao to collate and publish that edition; at his father Zhao Yongxian's urging he combined it with the *Jingui Yaolüe* as the *Zhongjing quanshu*.
- After printing, he obtained a Song-edition *Shanghan lun*, learned that Cheng Wuji's edition was not the complete text, and printed it as well. This is the main source of the text transmitted today as the "Song edition of the *Shanghan lun*."
- It criticizes the medical world of the day for favoring later schools such as Li Dongyuan and Zhu Danxi and neglecting the classics.
- "Healing the relation of father and son by memorials" is interpreted as referring to his father Zhao Yongxian's impeachment of Zhang Juzheng for "duoqing" (remaining in office instead of observing mourning).
- The translation stays close to the literal text; allusions (copying errors likened to confusing similar characters, Zhao Kuo who read only his father's military books without understanding the changes of war, etc.) are supplemented in brackets or parentheses.
